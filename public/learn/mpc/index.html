A lap is an optimization problem

On trackA race engineer's brief fits on a sticky note: be as fast as possible, stay on the track, don't hit anyone, follow the rules, and don't ask the car for more than it has. The figure above solved a small version of it. It slid the car's line across the track until the lap stopped getting faster, without ever leaving the asphalt.

In mathWrite the brief in symbols and you have an optimization problem. Each phrase becomes one piece of math. Hover or tap a line on either side to see its partner, and where it lives on the track.

Every optimization problem has the same three parts, and racing already gave you all three:

Decision variables
What you choose: the steering and throttle at every step
Objective
One number to make small: lap time, or tracking error
Constraints
What must hold no matter what: physics, track, rules, hardware

Lecture 12 asks this question about the controller, the part that turns a planned racing line into steering and throttle commands. It builds up to the full brief in stages: first with no limits at all (LQR, today), then with limits (MPC, Lecture 13).

In plain words

An optimization problem is three things written down precisely: what you may choose, the one number you want as small as possible, and the rules that must never break. Racing hands you all three before any math: steering and throttle, lap time, and physics plus the track.

The drivers you have built so far

On trackYour car already drives itself. Pure pursuit steers toward a point on the path ahead, and a PID loop turns an error into a steering command. You tuned both the same way: change a number, run the car, watch, repeat. The lookahead distance, the gains Kp, Ki, Kd and the speed table were all judged by eye.

\[ u(t) = K_p\, e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(t')\,dt' + K_d\, \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]

That gets a car around a track. On a race car it hits three walls:

  • One error in, one command out. PID is single-input single-output (SISO). A car has two inputs, steering and acceleration, and their effects are coupled: a steering angle that is fine at 2 m/s flips the car at 8 m/s. Two PID loops side by side don't know about each other.
  • No limits. PID will happily command a steering angle of π/2, or more acceleration than the motor has. An F1TENTH car's wheels stop at about ±24°. The formula doesn't know that.
  • No definition of best. Gains are judged by eye. With no number that says one tuning beats another, there is nothing to optimize.

In mathWhat we want instead is a controller that is MIMO (all states in, all inputs out), constraint-aware, optimal by a stated measure, and derived from the Lecture 6 model instead of tuned by hand. Today gets three of the four. Lecture 13 adds the limits.

Scoring a driver

On trackAfter an overtake, your car sits 1 m to the left of the racing line, pointing straight ahead at 5 m/s. Bring it back. Two drivers try: PD gains you might tune by hand, and a controller computed from a score. Before you can call either one better, you need the score.

In mathThe score is called the cost J, and lower is better. Build it one step at a time with the buttons above the figure. Each step fixes a flaw in the one before.

  1. One number per step. The lateral error e(k) is the signed distance to the line, one value per control step. Adding them up fails: a car that weaves left and right scores near zero.
  2. Square it. e(k)² has no sign, and a 2 m miss costs four times a 1 m miss, which is how a race engineer feels about it too.
  3. Weigh every state. Heading and speed errors matter too. Stack all the errors into x − xref and weigh them with Q = diag(Q1, …, Qn) ⪰ 0. Each weight acts like a P gain on its error, and a zero weight means “don't care”. The cost is now a bowl in the error.
  4. Price the steering. Without it the best driver steers infinitely hard (try LQR at step 3). Add u⊤Ru with R ≻ 0, so every bit of steering costs something. Now tracking and effort trade off.
  5. Count where you end. Split the score into a running cost q(x, u), paid at every step k = 0, …, N−1, and a terminal cost p(x(N)), paid once at the end of the horizon, where no input is left to choose. Its weight PN says how much the final state matters.
\[ q(x, u) = (x - x_{\text{ref}})^\top Q\, (x - x_{\text{ref}}) + u^\top R\, u, \qquad p(x) = (x - x_{\text{ref}})^\top P_N\, (x - x_{\text{ref}}) \]

Now try to beat the LQR driver with your own Kp and Kd. You can get close, but you can't win: LQR is not a well-tuned driver. It is the input sequence that minimizes this exact score, computed from the model. The LQR section shows how.

In plain words

The cost is the referee. It turns “drove well” into one number, so two drivers can be compared and a computer can search for the best one. The weights are where you say what you care about: Q for staying on the line, R for going easy on the steering.

The decision is the whole plan

On trackPit stop. Your car comes into the pit lane at 10 m/s, 15 m short of its box, and has to stop in the box. You control only the acceleration, at most 3 m/s² either way, chosen every 0.1 s for 10 s. That is 100 numbers. Draw them.

In mathYou just solved an optimal control problem by hand. Given the start x(0), the model and the reference, choose the whole input sequence U0 = [u(0), …, u(N−1)] to make the cost small:

\[ J\big(x(0), U_0\big) = p\big(x(N)\big) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} q\big(x(k), u(k)\big) \]
\[ \min_{U_0} \; J\big(x(0), U_0\big) \quad \text{subject to} \quad x(k+1) = f\big(x(k), u(k)\big), \;\; k = 0, \dots, N-1, \qquad x(0) \text{ given} \]
  • The unknowns are the inputs, all of them. That is N·m numbers for m inputs (here 100 × 1). Positions and speeds are not free: the dynamics constraint fixes them once the inputs are chosen. That is why moving one bar in Figure 3 changes the whole trajectory after it.
  • The decisions are sequential. J is a sum over time, and every step depends on the one before. That is the same structure as a shortest-path problem from Lecture 11.
  • The answer is a plan and a price: the optimal sequence U0* and the optimal cost J*. The best plan in Figure 3 came from a solver; the last section opens it up.

Two details make this concrete. The controller runs at a fixed rate, one measurement and one command every Δt, so time is counted in steps k. The Lecture 6 model is continuous, ẋ = f(x, u), and one Euler step turns it into x(k+1) = x(k) + Δt f(x(k), u(k)). The pit stop uses the simplest car model there is, a double integrator: position p measured from the box, speed v, input acceleration a.

\[ x = \begin{bmatrix} p \\ v \end{bmatrix}, \quad u = a, \qquad x(k+1) = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} x(k) + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^2/2 \\ \Delta t \end{bmatrix}}_{B} u(k), \qquad \Delta t = 0.1\ \text{s} \]
In plain words

You never choose where the car goes. You choose what you do, and physics decides where the car ends up. That is why the unknowns are only the inputs, and why changing one early input moves the whole rest of the trajectory.

Plan backward from the finish

On trackSuppose the fastest lap passes through Turn 3. Then its piece from Turn 3 to the finish must itself be the fastest way from Turn 3 to the finish. If a faster one existed, you would splice it in and beat your “fastest” lap. So a driver who knows the best time-to-finish from every spot on the track can drive optimally from anywhere, even after a spin.

In mathThat is Bellman's principle of optimality (1957): every tail of an optimal path is optimal. Optimal planning and optimal control share it. In Lecture 11 you computed the cost-to-come, forward from the start (Dijkstra, A*, the rewiring in RRT*). Control runs the same idea backward and computes the cost-to-go, the lowest cost from state x at step k to the end:

\[ J^*_k(x) = \min_{u(k),\dots,u(N-1)} \Big[\, p\big(x(N)\big) + \sum_{j=k}^{N-1} q\big(x(j), u(j)\big) \Big], \quad x(k) = x, \qquad J^*_N(x) = p(x) \]

Peel off the first step and what remains is J*k+1 again. That one line is the Bellman equation: the immediate cost plus the best cost from wherever you land.

\[ J^*_k(x) = \min_{u} \Big[\, q(x, u) + J^*_{k+1}\big(f(x, u)\big) \Big] \]

The controller is whichever input achieves that minimum. It is a feedback law: a function of the current state, not a fixed list of commands. Dynamic programming solves it backward, from k = N to k = 0.

\[ u^*_k(x) = \arg\min_{u} \Big[\, q(x, u) + J^*_{k+1}\big(f(x, u)\big) \Big] \]

Discretize the states and inputs and the recursion becomes a graph problem you can run by hand. States are nodes, inputs are edges, and the running cost q is the edge cost. Shortest path and optimal control are now the same problem.

More on dynamic programming

The controller is a lookup. All the work happens offline. Online, the car reads the arrow for the cell it is in. Knock it into any other cell and it simply reads that cell's arrow: no replanning. Drag the car in Figure 4 to see it.

Value iteration needs no horizon. After k sweeps from J = 0, each cell holds min(k, true cost) on an all-tarmac map, because every sweep lets information travel one more step. It stops when a sweep changes nothing: J is then a fixed point of the Bellman equation.

Policy iteration (backup slides) alternates two moves: evaluate the current controller exactly (a linear system), then improve it greedily. It takes few, expensive iterations; value iteration takes many cheap ones. Both need the full model and both enumerate every state.

Close is not cheap. On the Hairpin map the finish is two cells from the car as the crow flies, but the cost-to-go counts the way around. On Gravel shortcut, look for the boundary where cutting through the gravel stops paying off.

Ties. When two inputs give the same total, both are optimal. The arrows pick the first of up, down, left and right, so the car's path is one of several equally good ones.

The catch: the curse of dimensionality

On trackThat grid had 25 cells. A real car needs at least position, heading and speed, and a grid has to cover every combination of them.

In mathBellman, who coined the phrase “curse of dimensionality”, also knew the way out: don't store J as a table. If J*k can be written as a formula with a few numbers in it, the recursion only has to update those numbers. That happens exactly when the dynamics are linear and the cost is quadratic.

In plain words

Cost-to-go answers one question: how much is left to pay from here to the finish if I drive perfectly from now on? Know that number for every spot and driving becomes easy: always take the move that goes downhill in it. Working backward from the finish is how you fill the numbers in.

LQR: the bowl that stays a bowl

On trackNear the racing line the car's errors are small, and for small errors the car's response is close to linear: twice the steering, twice the turn. The score from Figure 2 is a bowl, quadratic in the error. Together those two facts make the Bellman recursion solvable with pencil and paper.

In mathStart with one state and one input. Regulation means xref = 0, so x itself is the error. A grid over all real x would need infinitely many cells.

\[ x(k+1) = a\,x(k) + b\,u(k), \qquad q(x, u) = Q x^2 + R u^2, \qquad p(x) = P_N x^2 \]

Take one step back from the end. The cost-to-go there is J*N(x) = PNx², so

\[ J^*_{N-1}(x) = \min_u \Big[\, Q x^2 + R u^2 + P_N\, (a x + b u)^2 \Big] \]

For a fixed x the bracket is a parabola in u, and since R > 0 it opens upward. Its minimum is where its slope is zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial u}\Big[\cdots\Big] = 2Ru + 2bP_N(ax + bu) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u^* = -K_{N-1}\, x, \qquad K_{N-1} = \frac{a b P_N}{R + b^2 P_N} \]

The best input is a gain times the state: a formula, not a table. Put u* back in and the cost-to-go is again a parabola in x, the same form as the terminal cost:

\[ J^*_{N-1}(x) = P_{N-1}\, x^2, \qquad P_{N-1} = Q + a^2 P_N - \frac{(a b P_N)^2}{R + b^2 P_N} \]

So the next step back looks exactly like this one. By induction, every step keeps one number Pk and one gain Kk, and no grid is ever stored.

Many states: the Riccati recursion

With n states and m inputs nothing changes but the bookkeeping. Guess that the cost-to-go is quadratic, J*k+1(x) = x⊤Pk+1x, minimize the quadratic in u, and substitute back:

\[ J^*_k(x) = \min_u \Big[\, x^\top Q x + u^\top R u + (Ax + Bu)^\top P_{k+1} (Ax + Bu) \Big] \]
\[ P_k = Q + A^\top P_{k+1} A - A^\top P_{k+1} B \big(R + B^\top P_{k+1} B\big)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A \]
\[ K_k = \big(R + B^\top P_{k+1} B\big)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A, \qquad u(k) = -K_k\, x(k) \]

This backward recursion is the Riccati recursion. For a long horizon Pk converges to one matrix P that solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE):

\[ P = Q + A^\top P A - A^\top P B \big(R + B^\top P B\big)^{-1} B^\top P A, \qquad K = \big(R + B^\top P B\big)^{-1} B^\top P A, \qquad u = -K x \]

That is the Linear Quadratic Regulator. Compute P and K once, offline. Online the controller is one matrix multiplication. For linear dynamics, a quadratic cost and no constraints it is globally optimal: no controller of any kind scores lower. And P is a bonus: the optimal cost from any start is J* = x⊤Px.

Q and R are the tuning knobs. A small R gives an aggressive controller with large inputs; a large R gives a gentle, slow one. You now tune what you care about, the weights, and the gains follow. Cost shaping replaces gain tuning.

Back to the car

The Lecture 6 model is not linear, but its error around the reference trajectory almost is. Linearize at every point of the reference and the error obeys a linear model whose matrices change along the track:

\[ e(k+1) \approx A_k\, e(k) + B_k\, \delta u(k), \qquad e(k) = x(k) - x_{\text{ref}}(k) \]

The backward recursion doesn't care that the matrices change. Run it with Ak and Bk and you get one tracking gain Kk for every point of the reference. The LQR driver in Figure 2 is exactly this, on a straight where A and B happen to stay constant.

In plain words

When errors are small and the cost is a bowl, the cost-to-go stays a bowl, so you only need its shape, one matrix P, instead of a giant table. The controller that falls out is a spring and a damper pulling the car back to the line, with their strengths picked for you by the weights. For the pit stop, K = [0.93, 1.40] is exactly that spring and damper.

When the car has limits

On trackLQR gave up three things the car cares about: the steering lock, the motor's acceleration limit, and the track edge. It also assumed linear dynamics. Put the limits back and see what breaks.

In mathTake the one-step problem from Figure 6 and allow only inputs in a box, u ∈ 𝒰 = [−ū, ū]:

\[ J^*_k(x) = \min_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \Big[\, q(x, u) + J^*_{k+1}\big(f(x, u)\big) \Big] \]

Bellman still holds, but J*k is no longer one quadratic, so the closed form is gone. Every further step back multiplies the pieces, and doing the recursion by hand stops being an option.

What is optimization?

On trackGo back to the race brief of Figure 1. Strip away the racing words and every version of it has the same shape. A shopping version: fill a bag with the most valuable items while keeping its weight under 15 kg. A racing version: make the cost small while respecting physics and limits.

\[ \min_{z} \; f(z) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(z) \le 0, \quad h(z) = 0, \qquad z = U_0 = \big[u(0), \dots, u(N-1)\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{Nm} \]

In mathHand that to a numerical solver, which searches over z directly: no recursion, no grid. The questions are speed and trust. The car needs an answer every 10 to 20 ms, and it needs the best answer, not just a good one. Here is a solver at work on the pit stop from Figure 3.

Two lessons. First, clipping the LQR answer is not the same as solving the constrained problem: the constrained optimum plans for the limit in advance and brakes at once. Second, the solver reaches the same answer from every starting guess. That is no accident: this problem is convex.

Why convex problems are the good ones

On trackOvertaking a slower car, you can pass on the left or on the right. Both are good, and which is better depends on details. A solver that starts on the left will polish the left pass and never look at the right one. That is what a non-convex problem looks like. A convex problem has no such forks: every downhill path ends at the same bottom.

  • Convex set: every point on the segment between two members is a member. A straight piece of track and the input box are convex; a hairpin is not.
  • Convex function: every chord between two points of its graph lies on or above the graph. Equivalently, the region above its graph (its epigraph) is a convex set.
  • Convex problem: a convex objective, convex inequality constraints and linear equality constraints. Then any local minimum is the global minimum, so a solver that can no longer go downhill has found the best answer. A large family of such problems has fast, reliable solvers.

Our problem is a QP

A quadratic cost, linear dynamics and box limits make the constrained pit stop, and the constrained tracking problem on the car, a quadratic program (QP) in z:

\[ \min_z \; \tfrac{1}{2} z^\top H z + g^\top z \quad \text{subject to} \quad G z \le h, \quad A_{\text{eq}}\, z = b_{\text{eq}} \]

Dedicated QP solvers such as OSQP solve problems of this size in well under a millisecond, which makes optimization-based control practical at 50 to 100 Hz. The 40-line solver of Figure 9 took a few milliseconds in your browser. Solve it once per time step, from the measured state, and you have Lecture 13.

More on solvers and on non-convex limits

Unconstrained convex problems are solved by gradient descent and its faster relatives (momentum, Newton's method). Figure 9 adds one move, the projection back into the box, which is cheap because a box projection is just a clip.

General constraints are usually handled by interior-point methods: replace each constraint gi(z) ≤ 0 by a log barrier −log(−gi(z)) that blows up at the boundary, solve, shrink the barrier, repeat. The minimizers trace the central path to the constrained optimum. Active-set methods and ADMM (the method inside OSQP) are the other common choices.

The track is not convex. Look at the hairpin in Figure 10. MPC on a race car handles this by linearizing the track edges around the car at each step, turning them into half-planes, and half-planes are convex. The nonlinear car model gets the same treatment: linearize around the current plan, solve a QP, repeat.

Why a QP is so fast here: the dynamics make H and the constraints banded in time, and solvers exploit that structure. Solve time grows roughly linearly with the horizon N.

In plain words

A limit you ignore while planning costs you later: clipped LQR brakes late because it plans as if it could brake harder. Planning with the limit built in fixes that. Convexity is what makes it trustworthy: the problem has one valley, so the solver's answer is the best one, not just a good one.

Next: model predictive control

Lecture 13 combines the two halves of today. From optimal control it takes the cost, the model and the horizon; from optimization, the constraints and a QP solver. The recipe is called receding-horizon control:

  1. Measure the state.
  2. Solve the constrained problem over the next N steps.
  3. Apply only the first input u*(0).
  4. Shift the horizon one step forward and repeat.

Lecture 13 covers how to set up the QP for real-time execution, why a problem that was solvable at one step stays solvable at the next (recursive feasibility), stability guarantees, and MPC on the F1TENTH car. After that, Lecture 14 adds uncertainty (stochastic optimal control and MPPI), and the module ends where control meets learning.
